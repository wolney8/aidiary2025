Public Platform Architecture and Productisation Plan

Act as a senior software architect, SaaS security engineer, privacy engineer, mobile architect, and technical product manager.

The current project is an AI-assisted diary and dream-journal application. Your task is to assess the existing repository and create an actionable plan for turning it into a secure, reliable, commercially viable public web and mobile platform.

Do not immediately rewrite the application or introduce large architectural changes.

First inspect the existing codebase, current framework, authentication, database models, encryption, AI integrations, deployment configuration, tests, documentation, and security controls. Clearly distinguish:

1. What already exists and works.
2. What exists but requires improvement.
3. What is missing.
4. What must be replaced before a public launch.
5. What can remain available for a private homelab deployment.

Core Product Goal

Create a web and mobile diary platform comparable in usability to applications such as Daylio, while maintaining its own focus on:

* Daily diary entries.
* Dream journalling.
* AI-assisted reflection.
* Mood and emotional tracking.
* CBT-informed exercises.
* Thought records.
* Personal insights and trends.
* Easy, low-friction entry creation.
* Secure synchronisation between web and mobile devices.

A user should be able to open the application, authenticate quickly, and begin writing an entry with minimal navigation.

3. Private Homelab Edition

Design a supported private edition that can run on the existing homelab without becoming the production platform.

The private edition should:

* Use containers.

* Avoid hard dependencies on public SaaS services.
* Use a supported database rather than treating SQLite as a scalable multi-user production database.
* Have documented backup and restore procedures.
* Remain compatible with the public edition where reasonably possible.

Clearly document where the private and public editions differ.

Treat diary entries, dreams, CBT records, mood data, and AI conversations as highly sensitive personal data.

Prepare a privacy architecture addressing:

* UK GDPR.
* Data Protection Act 2018.
* GDPR where EU users may be served.
* Data minimisation.
* Purpose limitation.
* Consent.
* Lawful basis.
* Records of processing.
* Data retention.
* Data portability.
* Right of access.
* Right to rectification.
* Right to erasure.
* International data transfers.
* Sub-processors.
* Data processing agreements.
* Age restrictions.
* Child accounts and parental consent.
* Cookie consent.
* Analytics consent.
* AI-processing consent.
* Privacy notices.
* Terms of service.

Do not state that diary entries are “completely private,” “confidential,” or “visible only to the user” unless that statement is technically and legally accurate.

Instead, design precise user-facing language explaining:

* How entries are encrypted.
* Whether AI providers receive entry content.
* Whether content is retained by AI providers.
* Whether content is used for model training.
* Which employees or administrators could technically access data.
* Under what exceptional circumstances access may occur.
* How backups are handled.
* How users can export or delete their information.

AI analysis must be optional and based on informed consent.

Where feasible, allow users to create entries without sending them to an AI service.

7. AI Safety and Crisis-Support Behaviour

The application is not a therapist, crisis service, medical device, or substitute for professional care unless a later regulated product process establishes otherwise.

Design an AI safety layer that can recognise possible indications of:

* Self-harm.
* Suicide.
* Immediate danger.
* Abuse.
* Severe emotional distress.
* Psychosis or loss of contact with reality.
* Risk to another person.

Do not rely on simple trigger-word matching alone. Trigger words may appear in dreams, quotations, fiction, historic accounts, or discussions about another person.

Use contextual classification with conservative escalation rules.

When risk is detected:

* Respond calmly and without judgement.
* Do not diagnose.
* Do not claim certainty.
* Encourage immediate human support where appropriate.
* Present location-appropriate emergency and crisis resources.
* Ask the user to confirm their country or region when it is not known.
* Make clear when emergency services should be contacted.
* Avoid manipulative engagement or emotional dependency.
* Do not hide, delete, or silently modify the user’s diary entry.
* Avoid repeatedly displaying crisis interventions after an obvious false positive.
* Record only the minimum safety metadata required.
* Do not expose sensitive safety classifications to unauthorised staff or analytics systems.

Regional support information must come from a maintained and verifiable source. Do not hard-code unverified telephone numbers into AI prompts.

Create a documented human-reviewed safety policy and test suite before release.

* Token revocation.

* API versioning.
* OpenAPI documentation.
* Webhooks where useful.
* Developer registration.
* Audit history.
* Data provenance.
* Duplicate detection.
* Entry source identification.
* Import and export formats.

Example scopes might include:

* entries:write
* entries:read
* moods:write
* dreams:write
* profile:read

Third-party applications must never receive a user’s password or unrestricted account access.

Also investigate compatibility with appropriate interoperability standards and common personal-data export formats, but do not claim compatibility until it is implemented and tested.

Implementation Rules

* Do not perform a big-bang rewrite.
* Do not introduce microservices without a demonstrated need.
* Do not use self-managed Kubernetes merely because it is familiar.
* Do not treat SQLite as the public multi-user production database.
* Do not place secrets in source control.
* Do not implement custom payment-card handling.
* Do not create custom cryptography.
* Do not claim medical or therapeutic efficacy without evidence.
* Do not claim absolute privacy unless it can be proven.
* Do not expose private entries in logs, analytics, crash reports, support tools, or administrator dashboards.
* Do not make destructive database changes without migrations, backups, and rollback procedures.
* Prefer incremental, testable changes.
* Preserve working functionality.
* Follow existing repository conventions unless there is a documented reason to change them.
* Mark assumptions explicitly.
* Cite official documentation for significant technical, security, payment, mobile-store, privacy, and infrastructure decisions.
